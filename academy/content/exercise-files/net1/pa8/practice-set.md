## Passage 6: The economy of attention

Information was once scarce and attention plentiful. A household might own a handful of books and receive a single newspaper, and each would be read with care. Today the situation is reversed. The quantity of text, images and video that can reach a person in a day far exceeds what anyone could absorb in a lifetime, while the number of waking hours has not changed. When one resource becomes abundant, another becomes scarce, and in a world of abundant information the scarce resource is attention.

This change has created a new kind of business. Many digital services are free to their users because the users are not really the customers; their attention is the product, sold to advertisers in small measured units. The more time people spend on a platform, the more it earns, and so platforms are designed, tested and continually adjusted to be difficult to put down. Notifications arrive at unpredictable intervals, feeds have no natural end, and content is selected by systems that learn which items keep each person looking longest.

None of this requires anyone to intend harm. Engineers are rewarded for increasing engagement, and engagement is easy to measure, whereas the effect of a product on a person's concentration, sleep or relationships is not. What is measured tends to be managed, and what is not measured tends to be neglected.

The costs fall partly on individuals, who may find sustained reading or reflection increasingly difficult, and partly on public life. Content that provokes outrage or fear is especially good at capturing attention, so systems that optimise for engagement may give it a prominence out of proportion to its accuracy or importance.

Remedies are being debated at several levels. Some place responsibility on users, urging them to switch off notifications and set limits on screen time. Others argue that individual willpower is a weak defence against products refined by thousands of experiments, and call for design standards or regulation. Between these positions lies a simpler observation: attention, like any valuable resource, is spent whether or not we decide how to spend it. The only question is who does the deciding.
